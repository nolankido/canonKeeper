CHAPTER I. THE LETTER FROM CAMBERWELL

It was upon a raw morning in the February of 1895 that I found Sherlock Holmes already at
breakfast, a letter propped against the toast-rack and his long fingers drumming upon the
cloth. He was, as I have had occasion to remark, a man of barely five feet and a half,
though so spare that strangers often took him for taller; and in the grey light of the
window his profile had that hawk-like keenness which I had learned to read as the prelude
to a case.

"You have been in Camberwell, Watson," said he, without looking up.

"I have not been out of the house."

"Then your boots have been, which comes to the same thing. There is a yellow clay upon the
left heel that I know of nowhere else in London." He tossed me the letter. "Read that, and
tell me what you make of it."

The letter was from a Mr. Josiah Pennell, bookseller, of Denmark Hill, and it set out in a
cramped and anxious hand that a ledger of accounts had vanished from his locked shop between
the Saturday evening and the Monday morning, that nothing else had been touched, and that
his assistant, a young man named Alfred Crane, had not been seen since.

"A ledger," said I. "Hardly the stuff of tragedy."

"A ledger is the stuff of every tragedy, Watson, if one reads it rightly. Mrs. Hudson!"
Our landlady appeared at the door. "We shall want a cab in ten minutes, and if Dr. Watson's
wife should call, you may tell her that I have carried him off to Camberwell and will
return him by dinner."

CHAPTER II. DENMARK HILL

Mary—Mary Morstan that was—had, in fact, called the evening before, and I had found her in the sitting-room with
her hands folded in her lap and the brown eyes that had first drawn me to her in the days
of the Agra treasure fixed upon the fire. She had news of her old governess, which need not
detain this narrative, and she bore my departure for Camberwell with the patience that was
the chief of her many virtues.

Pennell's shop was a narrow, dim place smelling of calf and dust. Holmes went through it as
a terrier goes through a rick, and within a quarter of an hour had found the mark of a
bootheel upon the sill of the back window, a smear of yellow clay upon the floor beneath
it, and, in the grate, a scrap of paper upon which the figures "£340" had been written and
then scored through.

"Your assistant, Mr. Pennell. Where does he lodge?"

"With his sister, sir, in Coldharbour Lane."

"And his character?"

"I would have trusted him with my life, sir, until Saturday."

"Men usually say that," said Holmes, "about the ones they have trusted with their money."

CHAPTER III. THE MAN FROM PENTONVILLE

The affair resolved itself, as Holmes's affairs so often do, into a single question asked in
the right place. The sister in Coldharbour Lane had a lodger, and the lodger had a history,
and the history led, by way of a pawnbroker in the Walworth Road, to a man whom I had not
expected to encounter again on this side of the grave.

"You will remember Jefferson Hope, Watson," said Holmes, as we rattled back toward Baker
Street. "The cabman. The Brixton Road business, in the first year of our acquaintance."

"I could hardly forget him."

"He is still in Pentonville, and he has written to me twice this year. It was he who put
me on to the pawnbroker, for the Walworth Road is a small world and the man Crane had been
boasting in it."

I confess that I stared. But Holmes had already turned to the window, and I let it pass.

CHAPTER IV. BAKER STREET

When we came in, Mrs. Hudson met us upon the stair with the news that a gentleman had
called and would not leave his name. He was waiting in our sitting-room, a stooped figure
in a rusty frock-coat, with hair as white as paper and the bowed shoulders of a man who has
spent fifty years over other people's books.

Holmes looked at him for a long moment, and then he laughed.

"Come, Watson, you have seen that gentleman before. The hair is powder, the shoulders are an
hour's practice before the glass, and the frock-coat is my own. I had the old bookseller's
part ready for Denmark Hill this evening, and Mrs. Hudson has been kind enough to let it
sit in my chair while I went out in my own character. It was necessary that Crane should
believe the shop watched by a stranger."

He pulled off the white wig as he spoke, and beneath it his hair was as dark as it had ever
been.

"And the ledger?"

"Will be back upon Mr. Pennell's shelf by morning, less three hundred and forty pounds,
which is what young Crane's lodger owed in the Walworth Road, and which I rather think
Crane will find it convenient to repay from his own wages over a period of years. It is not
a case for the police, Watson. It is a case for a bookseller who trusts his assistant a
little less and pays him a little more."
